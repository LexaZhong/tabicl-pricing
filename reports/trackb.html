Can TabICL price motor risk?

It ranks French motor risk well from 5,000 policies. It does not out-price a flat premium until 100,000. Those are different questions, and the evidence answers them differently.

Smallest training set where TabICL significantly out-prices charging every policy the same premium (deviance 88.1 vs 94.0, z = 3.1). At 5,000 the flat premium wins.

The metric had a degenerate solution

Why the first read of these results was wrong

The original evaluation ranked policies by predicted pure premium — loss per unit exposure — and traced cumulative loss against cumulative exposure. That statistic depends only on the ordering a prediction induces, so it is invariant to scaling by any positive constant. That invariance hides a trapdoor.

Let ŷ = c / e (predict a rate inversely proportional to exposure)
Then premium L̂ = ŷ·e = c — the same premium for every policy
But ordering by c/e = ordering by exposure, descending

Loss cost per unit exposure runs 917 (shortest-exposure decile) → 97 (longest)
⇒ that ordering scores Gini 0.4676 while carrying no risk information.

A model charging every customer an identical premium — zero differentiation — scored 0.4676. Two mechanisms produce the 9.4× gradient it exploits: attrition, since a policy cancelled after a total-loss claim has small exposure and a large claim; and the arithmetic of y = L/e, which throws short-exposure claimants into the extreme right tail.

The tuned GBM fell into it

At N ≤ 5,000, Optuna selected min_child_weight values under which neither of xgb_hurdle's boosters split — every tree one leaf, so p̂ ≡ p₀ and Ŝ ≡ S₀. Its Gini came out 0.46760699, bit-identical across five seeds and four training sizes, matching the trivial reference exactly. Its deviance differed (95.99 vs 100.28) because deviance is level-sensitive and Gini is not. Identical Gini with differing deviance is the fingerprint of a collapsed model, and it is now an automated check.

Ranking instead by predicted total loss (ŷ·e) removes the trapdoor: a constant premium ties across every policy, the Lorenz curve becomes a straight line, and the Gini is exactly zero. Every number below uses that metric.

At N=5,000 the trivial model beats every fitted model, TabICL included. At 10,000 four models tie it within noise. At 20,000 TabICL leads by 1.35 — but with SDs of 3.08 and 2.27 across 20 draws that is z = 1.6, not significant. Only at 100,000 does the gap become real (z = 3.1).

The calibration warnings running through the sweep say the same thing from the other side: predicted-to-actual ratios of 0.39–0.45. The models rank the portfolio correctly and then price it at roughly half what it costs.

Where the models fail is the same place

Lift at full data · 10 equal-exposure buckets

The top decile costs 1,177 per unit exposure. TabICL predicts 462; the tuned GBM predicts 352 — 39% and 30% of what actually arrives. This single bucket produces the calibration gap. The dataset's largest claim is 4,075,401 against a portfolio loss cost of 167, and no amount of training data makes that predictable from twelve rating variables.

Stability is bounded by the tail, not the model

20 resample draws per rung

Twenty independent subsamples per training size, each refit and rescored, give the distribution rather than a point estimate. Exposure stratification worked almost perfectly — and the metrics still moved a great deal.

Mean exposure varies by 0.13% across draws; the stratification is doing its job. Loss cost still varies 2.6×, driven entirely by which large claims happen to land in the sample — at N=10,000 one draw caught a 1.4M claim. Five automated comparability flags fired, all on exactly those draws. No sampling scheme fixes this. It sets a floor on how precisely any model's performance can be measured at these sizes, and it applies equally to every model in the comparison.

Which model is steadiest

On rate Gini at N=20,000, standard deviations across 20 draws: TabICL 0.0136, xgb_hurdle 0.0269, xgb_tweedie 0.0288, glm_hurdle 0.0607, glm_tweedie 0.1011. TabICL is the steadiest at that rung — but not at 5,000, where its 0.115 SD on total-loss Gini trails xgb_tweedie's 0.085.

The GLM instability is the more striking finding. glm_tweedie at N=5,000 ranges from −0.043 to 0.439 depending only on which rows it saw. Running deterministic models at a single seed had concealed that completely — the fitter is deterministic, but the subsample is not.

What this means

The two-stage framing carries much of the original result. A hurdle-structured GBM lifts XGBoost from 0.514 to 0.596 weighted Gini on its own. The earlier 0.17 → 0.40 improvement changed the model and the framing together, so the two were confounded; the framing-matched control separates them.

Ranking and pricing must be reported separately. A lift chart shows ranking. Deviance and calibration show pricing. On this data they disagree by a factor of twenty in required sample size, and a POC that reports only Gini would conclude TabICL works at 5,000 policies.

Thin data is where TabICL is weakest, not strongest. Below 2,000 policies it is worse than predicting the portfolio mean. The regime where it genuinely leads is 5,000–100,000 — a mid-sized book or a small segment of a large one, not a startup portfolio.
